How the index is built

1
Every saleAll sales of NYC buildings with 6+ apartments since 2016, from the Department of Finance: 5,896 qualifying sales.
2
Only rent-stabilized buildingsMatched by tax lot to the State's 2024 registration list (47,198 buildings). 421-a tax-break buildings, co-ops and rooming houses are excluded.
3
Only real market salesPortfolio deals, single-apartment transfers and data errors are screened out. Every exclusion is published with its reason.
4
Like-for-likeA robust hedonic regression compares price per apartment while holding size, age, elevator, mixed use and neighborhood constant.
5
Never revisedEach new quarter is chained onto the published series, so past values never change.
6
Hard to moveNo single sale has ever changed a quarter by more than 0.9%. The settlement value filters out measurement noise.

Why it is built this way

Research behind the choices

  • Hedonic, not repeat-sales. Apartment buildings rarely sell twice in a decade, so a repeat-sales index (like Case-Shiller) would discard most sales. For indexes used to settle derivatives, hedonic indexes are much more stable under revision than repeat-sales ones (Clapham, Englund, Quigley & Redfearn 2006), and they signal turning points sooner (Shimizu, Nishimura & Watanabe 2010). Hill (2013) surveys the method.
  • Rolling window, chained. This is the rolling-window time-dummy method of the IMF/Eurostat Handbook on Residential Property Price Indices. Revision is a key risk for index derivatives (Deng & Quigley 2008), so published values are never changed.
  • Filtering a thin market. Indexes built from scarce transactions are noisy. Tradable commercial real-estate indexes handle this with signal extraction and frequency conversion (Bokhari & Geltner 2010; Bourassa, Cantoni & Hoesli 2017). The settlement value uses a Kalman filter for the same purpose.

Why rent-stabilized buildings

  • Rent rules change what a building can earn, so they change what it is worth. When rent control ended in Cambridge, MA, the value of formerly controlled buildings and their neighbors rose sharply (Autor, Palmer & Pathak 2014). When San Francisco expanded rent control, landlords reduced rental supply (Diamond, McQuade & Qian 2019).
  • New York's 2019 Housing Stability and Tenant Protection Act limited rent increases on stabilized apartments. The index shows stabilized buildings falling from that quarter on, while market-rate rental buildings did not.
  • Cross-check: Ariel Property Advisors reports stabilized building prices of $270K per unit in 2019 and $163K in 2025 (−40%). The index's levels are about $263K and $159K–$169K.

Governance

The index follows the structure of the IOSCO Principles for Financial Benchmarks (2013): governance, benchmark quality, methodology quality and accountability.

  • Transparent methodology: the full method and code are public, and every number can be recomputed from the published inputs.
  • Data sufficiency: a quarter is published only with at least 40 qualifying sales. The minimum so far is 51.
  • Changes: method changes are announced at least one quarter ahead, and filter parameters are re-estimated at most once a year, with notice.
  • Corrections: published values are not revised. A data error is corrected with a dated notice, and the original value is kept for the record.
  • Audit trail: each release records its code version and input checksums.
  • Questions and complaints: open an issue on GitHub.
This is a research preview. It has not been independently audited, and no administrator has been formally designated under any benchmark regulation.

Limitations

  • Stabilization status comes from the 2024 registrations. Buildings that left stabilization before then, mostly before 2019, are missing from the early years.
  • Portfolio deals are excluded because the city records only their total price, not a price per building.
  • Sales are dated by deed, so the index trails deal-making by one to three months.
  • The model cannot see the share of apartments that are stabilized, the rent roll or building condition. These drive value, so quarterly estimates are noisy (about ±7%).
